Sapphic Romance Tropes: What They Are and Where to Start

Tropes are the promises a romance makes about how the love story will unfold. Here are the sapphic romance tropes readers search for most, what each delivers, the common pitfalls, and the best place to find books for each one.

A trope is a recognisable story pattern. In romance, tropes aren't clichés to avoid; they're the reason readers pick up a book. Someone who loves enemies to lovers isn't looking for a surprise structure. They want the banter, the slow reveal of respect, the moment the insults turn into something softer, and they want it done well.

Sapphic romance uses the same tropes as the rest of the genre, but some carry different weight between women. An age gap or a boss/employee dynamic plays differently without the gender power imbalance of a male-female pairing. Friends to lovers carries the specific fear of misreading a close friendship between women. And the ice queen has become a sapphic signature in its own right.

How common is each trope?

Lesbian Romance Reads tagged 116 recent sapphic novels for its State of Sapphic Romance 2026 report. Slow burn appeared in 88% of the books and mafia romance in more than a fifth. Its trope glossary defines 15 tropes with frequency data, if you like numbers.

Slow burn

The promise: a long build-up of tension, with the first kiss arriving late, often past the halfway mark. Why readers love it: anticipation and emotional payoff. Watch out for: books that confuse "slow" with "nothing happens". Good slow burns escalate.

Enemies to lovers (and rivals to lovers)

The promise: two women who clash, through rivalry, opposing goals or genuine dislike, gradually earn each other's respect and fall. Variants: rivals to lovers (competitive but not hostile), workplace rivals, opposite sides of a war. Watch out for: cruelty that never gets accounted for. Eikna Romance explains the difference in enemies vs rivals.

Read the full enemies to lovers sapphic guide.

Friends to lovers

The promise: a long friendship becomes a romance, with all the history that implies. Variants: best friends, childhood friends, bandmates, roommates. Watch out for: a lack of tension. The best ones make you feel the risk.

Read the full friends to lovers sapphic guide.

Age gap

The promise: a romance between women with a significant age difference, usually ten years or more, exploring experience, power and vulnerability. Often combined with boss/employee or ice queen. Watch out for: how the book handles power; the best ones take it seriously.

Read the full age gap lesbian romance guide.

Ice queen

The promise: a cold, controlled, often powerful woman (CEO, editor, surgeon, queen) thaws for exactly one person. It's one of the most distinctly sapphic tropes, with an entire Goodreads list devoted to it. Lee Winter (The Brutal Truth, Breaking Character), Roslyn Sinclair (The Lion Tamer), G Benson (Who'd Have Thought) and Lola Keeley (The Music and the Mirror, set in a ballet company) are the names to know.

Jossef S. has a full guide: sapphic ice queen romance books.

Fake dating

The promise: two women pretend to be a couple, for family, careers, a wedding or publicity, and the pretence stops being pretend. It's built-in proximity plus built-in deadline. Classic picks include Alexandria Bellefleur's Written in the Stars and Ashley Herring Blake's Iris Kelly Doesn't Date; 2026 added Kimani Mae's basketball romance Half Court Shot.

More: sapphic fake dating books and fake dating on Eikna Romance.

Forced proximity

The promise: the characters can't escape each other: a snowed-in cabin, a road trip, a shared hotel room, a single bed. Often stacked with enemies to lovers. Alison Cochrun's Here We Go Again traps two former best friends in a car for a cross-country trip.

More: lesbian forced proximity romance and the beloved sub-trope only one bed.

Second chance

The promise: exes or lost loves reunite, with older, wiser selves and old wounds to work through. 2026 examples include Amy Spalding's In Her Spotlight and Lisa Peers's Motor City Love Song.

More: sapphic second chance romance.

Workplace and boss/employee

The promise: office politics, professional stakes and the thrill of keeping it secret. Combines naturally with ice queen and age gap. Bellefleur's Playing for Keeps (rival publicists) and Blake's Get Over It, April Evans (resort coworkers) are 2026 examples.

More: sapphic workplace romance.

Mafia and dark romance

The promise: danger, morally grey heroines, loyalty tested by violence. Sapphic mafia romance has grown fast, especially on Kindle Unlimited. Jossef S.'s Matelda: In Silence We Forgive sets secrets, blackmail and Matelda's love for Mia against the loyalties of the Salvatore crime family; the Salvatore family guide maps the world.

Trope stacking

Most sapphic romances combine two or three tropes. Common stacks include age gap + ice queen + workplace, enemies to lovers + forced proximity, and slow burn + friends to lovers. Lesbian Romance Reads covers popular combinations such as age gap slow burn. Frequently asked questions

What is the most popular sapphic romance trope?

Slow burn. Lesbian Romance Reads found it in 88% of the 116 sapphic novels it analysed for its 2026 report. Enemies to lovers, age gap and ice queen are also among the most searched.

What is the ice queen trope in lesbian romance?

An ice queen is a cold, controlled, often powerful woman who slowly opens up to one person. It's one of the most distinctly sapphic romance tropes.

What is trope stacking?

Trope stacking means combining several tropes in one book, such as age gap with ice queen and workplace, or enemies to lovers with forced proximity.

What is the difference between enemies to lovers and rivals to lovers?

Enemies genuinely dislike or oppose each other at the start. Rivals compete but usually respect each other. Eikna Romance has a guide explaining the difference.
