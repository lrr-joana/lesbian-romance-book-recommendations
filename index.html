Why star ratings fail romance readers

A 4.3-star average tells you a book is popular. It doesn't tell you whether the romance is the main plot or a subplot, whether the sex is on the page, whether the ending is happy, or whether there's a cheating storyline you'd rather avoid. Two readers can rate the same enemies-to-lovers book one star and five stars for exactly the same reason: the heroines are cruel to each other for 200 pages.

That's why the most useful romance recommendations answer a short list of practical questions first:

  • What's the core trope? Fake dating, second chance, forced proximity, grumpy/sunshine and so on. Tropes are promises about the shape of the story.
  • How much heat? From kisses only to explicit. See the heat level scale.
  • What's the ending? Genre romance promises a happily ever after (HEA) or happy for now (HFN). Fiction with a romance in it makes no such promise.
  • What's the emotional temperature? Cozy, angsty, funny, dark, devastating.
  • Who is on the page? Pairing, ages, identities, culture, disability, body type.
  • What should you be warned about? Grief, abuse history, infidelity, violence.

Every recommendation on this site is framed around those questions.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best way to find a romance book I'll actually like?

Choose by trope, heat level and mood before you look at ratings. Decide whether you want a guaranteed happy ending, how explicit you want it, and which relationship dynamic you enjoy, then use a site that tags books by those details, such as Eikna Romance or Lesbian Romance Reads.

What is the difference between lesbian, sapphic and WLW romance?

Lesbian romance centres lesbian characters. Sapphic is the wider umbrella for any romance between women, including bisexual and pansexual women. WLW means women loving women and is used almost interchangeably with sapphic. Our WLW guide explains the terms in detail.

Do romance novels always have a happy ending?

Genre romance does by definition: the central couple ends together, either happily ever after (HEA) or happy for now (HFN). Books marketed as fiction with a love story, such as The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo, don't have to.

Can AI recommend romance books accurately?

General chatbots often invent titles or mismatch tropes. Tools that ground recommendations in a verified book database, like EIKNA's Lyra assistant, are far more reliable. Always check the book's listing before buying.
