WLW Books Explained: A Guide to Women Loving Women in Fiction

WLW, sapphic, F/F, lesbian, queer: book listings use all of them, often for the same books. Here's what each label means, how to read representation tags, and where to find WLW books in romance and every other genre.

WLW stands for "women loving women". In books, it's shorthand for any story where a central romantic or sexual relationship is between women, whether those women identify as lesbian, bisexual, pansexual or queer, cis or trans. You'll see it on BookTok, in Goodreads shelves, in Kindle keywords and in reading lists like ours.

The label exists because "lesbian" doesn't cover everyone. A romance between a lesbian and a bisexual woman is WLW and sapphic, but calling it a "lesbian romance" erases one of the two leads. WLW and sapphic are umbrella terms that make room for both.

Readers use these interchangeably when searching, and so do retailers. If you're looking for books, search all of them: "lesbian romance" brings up different results on Amazon than "sapphic romance" does on Goodreads.

WLW romance vs WLW fiction

This is the distinction that matters most for avoiding disappointment.

  • WLW romance is genre romance: the love story is the main plot and the couple ends together. See our lesbian romance books guide.
  • WLW fiction is any book with a significant relationship between women, in any genre. Fantasy, horror, literary fiction and memoir all count, and none of them promise a happy ending.

Lesbian Romance Reads draws this line clearly: its WLW books guide lists books where the love story isn't the whole plot, and every review states how much of the story is romance and how it ends.

Reading representation tags

Good review sites tag representation so you know who's on the page before you buy. Common tags include:

  • Identity: lesbian, bisexual, pansexual, asexual spectrum, trans, nonbinary love interest.
  • Gender expression: butch, femme, butch/femme, butch/butch. See butch lesbian romance.
  • Age: women over 40, late bloomers, age gap. See mature lesbian romance.
  • Race, culture and faith: see diverse sapphic books and Black lesbian books.
  • Coming out: some readers want it, others want books where identity is never the conflict. Tags help either way.

A very short history of WLW books

Sappho's fragments, written around 600 BCE on the island of Lesbos, gave us both "sapphic" and "lesbian". Radclyffe Hall's The Well of Loneliness (1928) is often called the first explicitly lesbian novel in English, and it ended in sacrifice. Mid-century pulp paperbacks reached huge audiences but were usually forced to end in tragedy or heterosexual marriage. Patricia Highsmith's The Price of Salt (1952) was a rare exception with a hopeful ending.

From the 1970s, small feminist and lesbian presses such as Naiad, and later Bella Books and Bold Strokes Books, built a dedicated readership. The ebook and Kindle Unlimited era opened the door to indie authors, and since around 2020 big publishers have competed for sapphic romance. Jossef S. covers the genre's evolution in What Is Sapphic Romance?

Frequently asked questions

What does WLW mean in books?

WLW stands for women loving women. It describes any book where a central romantic relationship is between women, regardless of whether they identify as lesbian, bisexual, pansexual or queer.

Is sapphic the same as lesbian?

Not exactly. Sapphic is an umbrella term covering all women attracted to women, including bisexual and pansexual women. Lesbian refers to women exclusively attracted to women, though readers often use both as search terms.

What does F/F mean in romance?

F/F means a female/female pairing, the same way M/F means male/female and M/M means male/male.

Do WLW books always have happy endings?

WLW romance does; WLW fiction in other genres doesn't have to. Check a review that states the ending if it matters to you.

What was the first lesbian novel?

The Well of Loneliness by Radclyffe Hall, published in 1928, is often cited as the first explicitly lesbian novel in English.
